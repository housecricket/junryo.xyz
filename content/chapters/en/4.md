# Chapter 4 – Selling an Idea

## The Last Minute of the Meeting

The idea came to Thắng Tất on a Tuesday evening, while he was rereading the “Why” page for the fifth clinic.

Asked why they had rented agents, all five doctors gave almost the same answer: “So I can sleep.” None of them said “to have a receptionist agent”. None of them said “to send automatic reminders”. And yet Hải Đăng’s sales page still laid agents out like goods on a shelf: an accounting agent, a receptionist agent, a customer care agent, each with its own price. A CEO working alone had to fit them together herself, like assembling a wardrobe from instructions printed too small to read.

He wrote one line in his notebook and underlined it twice: *What if we sold sleep, instead of selling agents?*

That weekend was the monthly all-company meeting. Twelve people, all of Hải Đăng, filled the big meeting room. By custom, the last fifteen minutes were set aside for “new ideas”, and anyone could say anything.

Those fifteen minutes went exactly the way they did every month. The head of sales spent seven minutes on a discount campaign. The product director spent five on the progress of Project Autonomy. The two of them argued for another two. With exactly one minute left, Thắng Tất raised his hand.

He stood up. His heart was beating so fast he could hear it in his ears. He had practised his opening line three times in front of the bathroom mirror.

“I think we should sell around what CEOs worry about, instead of selling one type of agent at a time. Take the clinics, for example. They don’t need a receptionist agent, they need…”

“Interesting,” the head of sales cut in, in a very friendly voice. “But if we sell by worry, that’s a package for every industry. How would that ever scale? Write it up and send it to me.”

A few people nodded. The host thanked everyone. Chairs scraped. The meeting was over.

Thắng Tất sat down, his cheeks burning. He hadn’t even got as far as the word “sleep”.

The meeting minutes, taken by an agent, appeared on the screen before anyone had even stood up. Neat, complete, no one left out. His contribution was summed up in a single line: *Proposal to sell based on needs. More data required.* The word “sleep” was nowhere to be found.

On the way back to his desk, he heard two of the new hires in the far row whispering to each other.

“I had an idea too. But after seeing Thắng Tất get cut off like that, forget it.”

> **Theory corner – Psychological safety**
>
> In 1999, Amy Edmondson published a study in *Administrative Science Quarterly* of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company. She defined *psychological safety* as a shared belief within a team that it is safe to speak up, ask questions, admit mistakes or put forward ideas without being embarrassed or punished. Teams with high psychological safety learned more and performed better. Years later, Google’s Project Aristotle, which studied more than a hundred of its own teams, also ranked this factor as the most important of all.
>
> No one in that meeting room meant any harm. The head of sales even said “interesting”. But one friendly interruption in front of the whole company was enough to make two new hires quietly put their own ideas away. Trung’s book says something similar: in a crowd, opinions tend to drift toward consensus, and the many breed the ordinary.

## A Finding and an Idea

Thắng Tất assumed Trung would help. Trung had been working longer than anyone else in the department, and everyone respected him. One word from him at the next meeting would settle it.

Trung heard the whole story, then shook his head.

“I won’t speak up for you.”

“Why not?”

“Because if I say it, people will listen because it’s me.” Trung poured the tea, slowly, until the cup was full. “The next time you have an idea, you’ll have to come looking for me again. And I won’t be here forever.”

He paused for a moment, then added, as lightly as if it were nothing:

“Besides, your idea isn’t ready to sell.”

Thắng Tất was a little stung. “It seems clear enough to me. Five doctors all said the same thing.”

“Five doctors saying the same thing is a finding. It isn’t an idea yet.” Trung pushed the cup across to him. “My book has a very practical passage. A good idea only means something once it’s put into practice, so you have to learn to sell it to the people who have the power to decide. And to sell it, you have to treat it like a project: look at the market, look at the circumstances, work out the path forward. In other words, you have to walk the whole road of making it feasible yourself, before you go and persuade anyone else.”

“But I didn’t even get to finish my sentence.”

“You had one minute, and you used it to tell a finding to eleven people who wanted to go home.” Trung looked at him, not reproachfully, just looking. “It wasn’t right of the head of sales to cut you off. But his question was a good one. A package for every industry: how does that scale? Do you have an answer yet?”

Thắng Tất said nothing. A thin thread of steam rose from the tea in front of him.

“There’s a shorter line in the book too,” Trung said. “These days, the hardest thing is finding the right problem. You’ve already done the hardest part. The rest is just work. And nobody can do it for you.”

## Four People and Two Friday Afternoons

Trung suggested he read another passage in the book. For new work, a small team, sometimes only five people, set apart from the daily routine and steered regularly by its leader, often produces results more distinctive than an entire crowd.

Thắng Tất’s team had four people. Lực Sỹ joined the moment he heard about it, without asking anything more. Ngọc Mai from sales said yes after hearing him say exactly three words: “selling sleep”. The fourth was Quân, the young man from the new hires’ row, the one who had quietly put his idea away at the meeting. Thắng Tất went to find him himself, sat down beside his desk, and asked: “What were you going to say that day?”

They had only Friday afternoons, for two weeks.

The first task was to answer the head of sales’ question. Ngọc Mai brought the two hundred most recent sales conversations. The team read every one, looking not for which features the CEOs asked about, but for what they were worried about. By the fiftieth conversation, they began to see a shape. By the hundredth, the shape was unmistakable.

Whether it was a clinic, a bakery or a tailoring workshop, the worries of people who worked alone came down to three things. Fear that something would happen while they weren’t there. Fear of losing money without knowing it. And fear of missing customers.

“So it’s not a package for every industry,” Quân said. It was the first time he had spoken up in the group, and his voice still trembled a little. “It’s three packages. Each industry just changes the script.”

Thắng Tất looked at him, then over at Lực Sỹ. That was exactly the answer he hadn’t had, in that last minute of the meeting.

In the second week, they tested the first package with twenty clinics on a free trial. Ngọc Mai named it “the Sound Sleep package”. There was nothing new in it, technologically. It was still the receptionist agent, the aftercare agent, the nine o’clock message, and the rules that sent urgent messages through to the doctor’s phone. The only difference was how the conversation opened. Ngọc Mai didn’t start with a list of features. She started with a question:

“Last week, how many times were you woken up in the middle of the night?”

The other end of the line usually went quiet for a moment. Some people laughed. Some sighed. One doctor said: “You ask me that, and I’m already sold.”

Thirteen of the twenty clinics signed up. Three times the sales team’s usual rate.

> **Theory corner – What do customers “hire” a product to do?**
>
> In 2016, Clayton Christensen, together with Taddy Hall, Karen Dillon and David Duncan, wrote *Know Your Customers’ “Jobs to Be Done”* in the *Harvard Business Review*. The central idea is that customers don’t buy products. They “hire” them to do a job in their lives. The most famous example is the morning milkshake: many people buy it not because it tastes good, but because it keeps them from getting bored and hungry on a long drive. Only by understanding that “job to be done” can you know which way to improve the product.
>
> The doctors weren’t hiring a receptionist agent. They were hiring a full night’s sleep with peace of mind about their patients. When Thắng Tất’s team looked through the lens of the “job to be done” instead of industry, hundreds of possible service packages shrank to three.

## Selling It a Second Time

This time Thắng Tất didn’t wait for the all-company meeting. Trung’s book said there are many ways to sell an idea: in a meeting, in a spare moment, or over a light-hearted story. He asked Ngọc Mai to set up lunch with the product director.

He brought two options. Option A was to switch the entire sales page to three packages built around worries. Option B was much smaller: offer only the Sound Sleep package to clinics for one quarter, measure the results, and only then decide what came next. Trung had once told him, in the tone of someone stating the obvious, that walking into a negotiation with only one option is an easy way to fail.

The product director listened to the end without interrupting once. Then he put down his chopsticks.

“Thirteen out of twenty is too small a sample. You chose clinics already on a free trial, which means people who were already well disposed. And you haven’t worked out, if this package sells well, how many urgent messages will hit doctors’ phones every night. A package called ‘sound sleep’ that wakes doctors up more often, and they’ll cancel next month.”

Thắng Tất felt his face grow hot again, just as it had at the meeting. But this time, in the middle of that heat, he remembered a line from the old book: as long as someone still criticizes you, you are still growing, so be grateful when they do.

“Thank you,” he said, and was surprised to realize he meant it. “I hadn’t thought of that third point. Could you give me another week?”

A week later, he came back with exactly one page. Lực Sỹ had rerun the data from the first five clinics: since the urgent-message rules went in, the number of times doctors were woken at midnight had dropped sharply, not risen. Quân had called ten more clinics that had never heard of Hải Đăng. Seven of them agreed to a trial.

When the product director finished reading, he didn’t mention Option A. He signed off on Option B.

The following month, at the all-company meeting, it was the product director himself who invited Thắng Tất up to speak for ten minutes. He spoke for eight. For the last two, he turned and handed the microphone to Quân, who had come up with the line “three packages, each industry just changes the script”. Quân stood up, the microphone trembling slightly in his hand, and this time nobody cut him off.

That evening, Trung sent him a photo of a page from the old book. The page described a place where anyone could say: “I’m an ordinary employee, but I have a brilliant idea.” Beneath the photo, Trung had added one line:

*Today you helped someone else say that.*

Thắng Tất read the line several times. Then he opened the meeting minutes the agent had taken. This time, Quân’s contribution had his name on it, on a line of its own.

> **Theory corner – The moves that matter when raising an issue with leadership**
>
> Jane Dutton and Susan Ashford spent years studying how employees “sell” an issue to leaders. In *Moves That Matter* (Dutton, Ashford, O’Neill and Lawrence, *Academy of Management Journal*, 2001), they identified three groups of moves common among those who sell successfully. The first is *packaging*: tying the issue to goals leaders already care about, and putting it in their language. The second is *involving others*: having allies before speaking up. The third is *choosing the right timing and the right channel*, including informal ones.
>
> Thắng Tất’s first attempt failed not because the idea was bad, but because he was missing all three moves. The second time, he had data that answered exactly what worried the product director, a small team standing behind him, and he chose a lunch instead of the last minute of a meeting.

## Thắng Tất’s Notebook

He wrote this page after the second all-company meeting:

1. **A finding is not yet an idea.** To sell it, you have to keep going: answer in advance the hardest question others will ask, test small, get numbers, and always bring an Option B.
2. **Crowds breed the ordinary. Small teams breed the different.** The last minute of a meeting is no place for an idea to be born. Four people and two Friday afternoons can be.
3. **Be grateful to those who criticize you, and make room for others to speak.** The product director’s criticism made the idea better. Two minutes given to Quân may matter more than my own eight.

In the margin, a small line: *Last time, I was the one who got cut off. If I hadn’t gone looking for Quân, I would have been part of the crowd that kept him silent.*

### Over to you

- In your meetings, who usually speaks last, and who has never spoken up at all?
- Are you holding on to a finding that hasn’t yet become an idea? What is the hardest question others will ask about it?
- What job are customers “hiring” your product or your work to do, beyond the features you usually present?
- The next time someone criticizes your idea, try saying “thank you” before you say “but”.
