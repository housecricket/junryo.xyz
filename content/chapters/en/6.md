# Chapter 6 – People Unlike Us

## A Question in the Middle of the Party

The party for eight thousand seven hundred was held right in the large meeting room, on a Friday afternoon. Nobody called it a party, because Hải Đăng wasn’t in the habit of celebrating. It was only that Hằng brought up two boxes of steamed rice cakes, Ngọc Mai ordered a few cups of milk tea, and Quân put the photo of the day-ninety whiteboard up on the screen, blown up so large that Trung’s small line in the bottom corner took up nearly half the wall.

People laughed a lot. It was the laughter of people who have just come a long way, legs still aching, but no longer worried.

Thư sat at the head of the table, near the door. She had joined the team only two weeks earlier, after twelve years as a claims adjuster for an insurance company. Trung was the one who had brought her in. He introduced her briefly at the Monday morning meeting, “Thư will be sitting with our team,” and nothing more, no further explanation. In those two weeks she had said very little. She read, she took notes, and every time she finished a document she folded down a tiny corner of a page, the way you mark a place you need to come back to.

When Quân raised his cup of milk tea, she raised her hand.

“May I ask a question?” Her voice was quiet and even, not at all the voice of someone out to spoil the party. “Of these eight thousand seven hundred companies, how many will still be here in six months?”

The meeting room went still for a beat. Quân lowered his cup.

“We haven’t thought as far as six months,” Thắng Tất said, trying to keep his voice cheerful. “We’ve only just finished ninety days.”

Thư nodded, as if that were exactly the answer she had been waiting for. “One more question. The Sound Sleep package promises that when there’s a dangerous case, the agent will call the doctor no matter what time it is. Has anyone checked whether the doctors actually pick up?”

This time nobody answered. Ngọc Mai looked down at the box of cakes. Someone quietly cleared their throat. The silence wasn’t heavy, but it spread very fast, like a drop of ink falling into a glass of clear water.

Trung sat at the back of the room, stirring his coffee. He said nothing at all.

## The Bad-Weather Forecast

The following week, Thư asked four more questions. Each one came right when everyone was happiest.

When Ngọc Mai showed off her new opening script for the bakeries, Thư asked what would happen if a shop owner misread the “losing money without knowing it” package as a warning that their money really was disappearing. When Quân reported that the satisfaction rate had gone up three points, she asked whether those three points were measured on the people who had stayed, or on everyone, including the ones who had left. When Phong presented how the agents recognized a customer’s worry on their own, she asked, “What does it look like when it gets it wrong?”

Phong looked at her for a moment, then said, “It doesn’t get it wrong.”

“Then you haven’t seen it get it wrong,” Thư said. Not harshly. Just the way you would correct a number.

By Thursday, Quân had given her a nickname, murmured in a corner of the pantry: “the bad-weather forecast department”. A few people laughed. Thắng Tất laughed too, then felt a little embarrassed that he had.

But, if he was honest, he was tired. For three months the team had run on a kind of energy that was hard to name, the energy of ten people believing in the same whiteboard. Each of Thư’s questions was like a hand quietly turning that flame down. He saw it on everyone’s faces after every meeting: a slight sag, a slight hesitation, a slight “let’s deal with it next week”.

On Friday evening he sat down to write a message to Trung. He wrote it three times over, whittling every word until it sounded as gentle as possible. The final version was just two sentences: *I think Thư would do better working on her own. Our team needs to keep its momentum.*

Trung replied ten minutes later, also with just one sentence:

*Are you sure the person who makes you uncomfortable isn’t the person you need most?*

## The Reasoning of People Unlike Us

On Monday morning, Trung said nothing about the message. He only set the old book down on Thắng Tất’s desk, already open to a page with pencil marks, and went back to his seat.

*A leader must know how to take in people unlike himself.*

Below it, in the same faded pencil, was a longer passage. Taking in people unlike you, the writer said, is not only kindness. Its root is understanding: understanding the reasoning of people unlike you, of lines of thought unlike your own. And because you understand, you know when to use them, and for what. The bigger the leader, the more people he must be able to take in. As many people as you can take in, that is how many you should lead.

Thắng Tất read the last sentence twice. Then he sat and counted, and he wasn’t pleased with the number he got.

The team had ten people, him included. Six had come because of the whiteboard: Phong, Hằng, the intern, the head of sales, and two engineers. The other four had been together since the Sound Sleep package. Each with a different trade, a different age, a different voice. But they were all alike in one thing: they liked building things that didn’t exist yet. They were people who looked at a hard problem and saw a path, not a cliff. The whiteboard was a magnet. And a magnet only attracts iron.

Ten people, and as it turned out, only one way of thinking.

He messaged Thư and asked whether she was free for a coffee. He didn’t bring his laptop. He remembered what Lực Sỹ had told him once: don’t bring a solution, just go empty-handed.

> **Theory corner – Diversity beats ability**
>
> In 2004, Lu Hong and Scott Page published a much-debated result in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. Using mathematical models and simulations, they showed that under certain conditions, a randomly chosen group of problem solvers with diverse approaches can outperform a group made up entirely of the best. The reason is that the best tend to be good in the same way, so they get stuck in the same places.
>
> In 2007, in his book *The Difference*, Page drew a clear distinction: what creates the strength is not differences in age or appearance, but differences in *how people see* and *how they solve*. The ten people on Thắng Tất’s team differed in a great many ways. Just not in the one that mattered most: they all looked at the problem from the builder’s side.

## The Bakery with the Fire Alarm

The café on the ground floor was empty. Thư ordered a hot tea and sat up straight, both hands on the table, like someone waiting to be interviewed.

“I’m sorry about the past few days,” Thắng Tất began. “Everyone’s been a bit…”

“It’s fine.” She smiled, the first time he had seen her smile. “I’ve had twelve years to get used to it. At my old company, my department was called the ‘yes, but’ department.”

He asked her why she had left insurance. She didn’t answer right away. She turned her cup of tea around once, then told him a story.

In her first year on the job, she was handed the claim of a small bakery that had burned down at three in the morning. The paperwork was all in order. The shop had a fire alarm installed, an inspection report from the installer, a signature and a stamp, the word *passed* printed in bold. She went to the site, stood in the middle of the still-warm ashes, and found the alarm, intact, on the ceiling. There was no battery inside.

“The owner said the alarm kept going off by mistake every time a batch came out of the oven. After a while he took the battery out. Meant to put it back one of these days.” Thư paused. “Everything had been checked. It was just that nobody checked whether it still rang.”

Thắng Tất sat very still.

“In insurance,” she went on, “you don’t sell a promise. You sell keeping that promise on the worst day. For twelve years, I only ever met customers on the worst day of their lives. So I got used to thinking backwards: if this goes wrong, how will it go wrong, at what time, and who will be the first to find out.” She looked at him. “Usually the first to find out is the customer. And by then it’s too late.”

“So why did you come here?”

“Because I got tired of meeting people on their worst day.” She took a sip of tea. “I wanted to try standing out in front, for once.”

Thắng Tất went back to his desk and opened the Sound Sleep package’s configuration sheet. He found the line that logged the status of urgent night-time calls. The system recorded exactly two words: *call placed*.

There was no column that said *answered*.

> **Theory corner – Groupthink**
>
> In 1972, the psychologist Irving Janis published *Victims of Groupthink*, an analysis of disastrous decisions in American foreign policy, such as the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961. He showed that close-knit, confident groups fresh from success often fall into a particular state: people tacitly avoid saying anything that goes against the grain, silence is taken as agreement, and whoever raises a doubt is seen as the one spoiling the team spirit.
>
> Janis didn’t blame stupidity. He blamed harmony. The closer the group and the more it wins, the more easily it loses the ability to hear what it doesn’t want to hear. Trung had once read the team a line with the same idea from the old book, back when Lực Sỹ stood up to oppose the change: when people succeed, they listen less to others.

## The Silent Phone

It took Phong a night to pull the data. He did it alone, without telling anyone, and sent the result at four in the morning, with one line that had no punctuation at all: *thư was right*

Of the more than two hundred clinics using the Sound Sleep package, thirty-one doctors set their phones to do-not-disturb from eleven at night. Over the past three months, the agent had made twenty-three urgent calls at night. Six of those calls rang in silence, on phones lying face down on bedside tables, in six dark rooms that no one on the team had ever set foot in.

The system logged all six as *call placed*. And the agent, exactly as it had been taught, messaged the patient: “The doctor has been notified.”

![The silent phone](Minh_hoa_Ch6_Chiec_dien_thoai_im_lang.jpg)

No one came to any harm. Five people took themselves to the emergency room, following the instructions. One waited until morning, then came into the clinic with one cheek badly swollen, and his doctor apologized again and again over a call she hadn’t even known had been made.

When Thắng Tất reached that line, he put his phone down. A chill ran down his spine, as familiar as the night he read the flower shop owner’s message. But this time it came with another feeling, a far more uncomfortable one. He remembered the party, Thư’s raised hand, and the silence spreading like ink in water. That question had been sitting on the table for nearly two weeks. The whole room had seen it. And the whole room had chosen to go on eating rice cakes.

There was only one thing he couldn’t understand: the first clinic. Dr. Diệp wanted to sleep after ten at night too; it was the first thing she had ever told him. And yet on the night she was called at one in the morning, she had picked up.

That afternoon, he asked to go down to the clinic. Thư went with him. At the last minute Trung put on his jacket and came along too, saying it had been a long time since he had been down to that market.

The clinic hadn’t changed. Still one dental chair, one instrument cabinet, a row of plastic chairs, and the smell of antiseptic that crept into your clothes. Dr. Diệp had just seen her last patient out the door. She saw Thắng Tất first and nodded hello. Then she saw the man standing behind him.

“It’s been a long time,” she said.

“Still the last one to switch off the lights?” Trung asked.

“Still.”

Thắng Tất looked from one to the other, a little surprised. “You know Dr. Diệp?”

“For a long time,” Trung said, and pulled up a plastic chair and sat down, like someone who had sat there many times before.

So Trung had had his teeth seen to here too, Thắng Tất thought. The clinic was near the market; everyone gets a toothache sometime.

Dr. Diệp opened her phone to show them. In the list of numbers allowed to ring through while the phone was on silent, the agent’s number sat on the very first line.

“I set it up myself, the first night,” she said. “I knew I’d be turning the ringer off. I hired it so I could sleep, not so it could call me and I wouldn’t hear.” She paused. “I assumed everyone knew to do that.”

Nobody knew. Not even Thắng Tất. That step wasn’t on the “Why” page, nor in the why column, because it had never been his job. It was the job of one careful doctor, done alone, on a night nobody saw. When the Sound Sleep package multiplied into two hundred clinics, whatever was on paper got copied over all two hundred times. A person’s habits did not.

> **Theory corner – Authentic dissent and role-played dissent**
>
> Many organizations know about groupthink and try to fight it with a role: they appoint someone as “devil’s advocate”, whose job is to argue against. In 2001, Charlan Nemeth, Keith Brown and John Rogers published an experiment in the *European Journal of Social Psychology* comparing two approaches. One group heard someone playing the role of dissenter. The other heard someone who genuinely thought differently.
>
> The results were clear: authentic dissent stimulated the group to come up with more solutions, and better ones. Role-played dissent had far less effect, and sometimes even made the group cling harder to its original view. People can tell at once which questions have been assigned, and which come from a real worry. Thư wasn’t playing a role. Twelve years of standing among the ashes had taught her to think that way.

## The What-If-It-Fails Column

That evening, the team fixed three things.

The agent was no longer allowed to say “the doctor has been notified” when the doctor hadn’t picked up. It could only say what it knew for certain: “I’m calling the doctor now. While you wait, please do the following right away.” If nobody answered after two minutes, it would call the second contact the clinic had registered, and tell the patient honestly that the doctor hadn’t picked up yet.

Every new clinic, on its first night, would get a test call at half past eleven. If the doctor didn’t answer, someone would call back the next morning and sit with her until the setting was done.

The third thing was Thư’s. She proposed adding a new column to the agent configuration sheet, right next to the why column. She called it the “what-if-it-fails” column. Every promise an agent made to a customer had to answer three questions: if this promise fails, how will it fail; what time will it be; and who will be the first to find out. If the answer to the third question was “the customer”, that promise was not yet allowed to be sold.

Quân was the one who filled in that column for the first three packages. He stayed until nine at night, and the next morning he went to Thư’s desk and set down a cup of hot tea. He said nothing about the nickname. Thư didn’t ask either.

That afternoon, Ngọc Mai stopped by Thắng Tất’s desk and said quietly, “At the party, when Thư asked, I was about to say ‘oh, come on.’ Good thing I didn’t.” She tilted her head and looked at him. “You were going to say the same, weren’t you?” Thắng Tất didn’t deny it. To be read that easily, and all he felt was relief.

At the end of the week, Thắng Tất went over to Trung’s desk. Trung was standing by the window, looking down at the street.

“I take back the message I sent the other day,” Thắng Tất said.

“I never took it as a request,” Trung said, without turning around. “I took it as a question. You were asking me whether you were allowed to work only with people like yourself.”

Thắng Tất was silent for a while. “The book says you should lead only as many people as you can take in. So until last week, I could only lead ten.”

Trung turned around and smiled. “It’s eleven now.”

The Friday after, the team celebrated again, this time for nine thousand. Quân raised his cup of milk tea, then stopped halfway and turned toward the head of the table.

“Thư,” he said, “before we drink, do you have a question?”

The whole room laughed. But this time, nobody looked down at the box of cakes. Everyone turned toward the door, and waited.

## Thắng Tất’s Notebook

He wrote this page after the afternoon at the clinic:

1. **The person who makes you uncomfortable may be the person you need most.** The hardest questions to hear usually come from a place you have never stood. Discomfort is only a sign that you have just touched a corner you hadn’t looked at.
2. **Taking people in starts with understanding the reasoning of those unlike you.** Not putting up with them, not giving in to them. Sit down, empty-handed, and hear out why that person thinks the way they do. Only once you understand will you know what to use them for.
3. **Right after a win is when you are most deaf.** Success makes people listen less to others. When the whole room is happy is when you need someone who dares to raise a hand and ask.

In the margin, a small line: *A magnet only attracts iron. If you want people unlike you, you have to go looking for them, then keep them.*

### Over to you

- On your team, who is the one who often asks questions that make the whole room go quiet? When was the last time you really sat down to understand why they ask them?
- If you count honestly, how many different ways of thinking does your team have, not how many people?
- Is there a promise your team is making to customers where the first to find out it has failed would be the customers themselves?
- At your team’s last celebration, was there a question that got skipped because nobody wanted to spoil the fun?
