# Chapter 7 – The Right People on the Bus

## Two Requests

The first request arrived on Monday morning, by email, worded very politely. Khoa, the intern who had once written “I don’t know what I can do, but I want to be near this work”, was asking to leave the team and go back to watching the customer support inbox. The reason he gave was that he wanted “to learn from the basics”.

The second request arrived on Tuesday afternoon, and not by email. Tùng, an engineer who had been with the team since the first week of the whiteboard, stood in front of Thắng Tất’s desk, hands in his pockets, and spoke fast, as if afraid he might change his mind:

“I want to quit. I can’t work with Phong anymore.”

Thắng Tất asked him to sit. Tùng didn’t sit.

“I finish writing a section, and the next morning I open it and it’s been rewritten. Nobody tells me. Nobody explains. When I ask, he answers in exactly one line: ‘The old one was slow.’” He stopped and took a breath. “The old one really was slow. The new one really is faster. I know. But for three months now I haven’t known what I came here to do.”

That evening, Thắng Tất opened Khoa’s email again and read it more carefully. On the very last line, after the thank-you, was a sentence he seemed to have written and then meant to delete, and forgotten: *I asked Phong a few times, and he told me to read the code and I’d understand.*

He looked at the two requests side by side on the screen. Two different people, two different ways of saying it, one and the same name.

Phong. The night-shift engineer who had worked out how to let the agents recognize a customer’s worry on their own, which was what made the number on the board jump from three thousand to eight thousand seven hundred. The one who had pulled the data alone at four in the morning and found the six calls that rang in silence. If he had to point to one person without whom the team would never have got where it was today, Thắng Tất would point to Phong without having to think.

And Phong was also the very person two colleagues were ready to leave rather than keep sitting next to.

## The Night-Shift Man

Since the ninety days, Trung had let Thắng Tất lead the team. He made no appointment and announced nothing. He never sat in on another Friday afternoon review, and whenever someone asked him about something to do with the team, he pointed toward Thắng Tất’s desk. Until one day, people went straight there on their own.

So this time, too, Thắng Tất went looking for the answer himself first.

He went through the change history in the system for the past three months. Phong had rewritten the work of four people, mostly between one and four in the morning. Every time, the new version was better than the old one. Every time, there wasn’t a single line of notes. He counted the times Phong had answered questions in the team’s shared channel: twenty-three, the longest of them eleven words.

He went to see Lực Sỹ, one of the longest-serving people at Hải Đăng, to ask what kind of person Phong was.

Lực Sỹ thought for a moment. “For three years Phong has worked the night shift alone,” he said. “On the night shift there’s nobody to ask. If something breaks, you fix it yourself, then you go to sleep, and whoever comes in the next morning just sees everything running.” He tapped the tip of his pencil lightly on the edge of the desk. “On the night shift, someone like that is the best there is. Nobody ever told him the day shift is different.”

“It was different back when we were upstairs from the phở shop,” Lực Sỹ went on. “The company had a custom: once a year, everyone had to change seats. I was in operations, then I moved to sales for half a year, then sat on the night shift for three months. Everyone called me the company’s utility knife. Wherever we were short, that’s where I went.” He laughed, a little embarrassed. “Once things started running smoothly, the custom just faded away. Phong has stayed in one seat, on one shift, for three years.”

Thắng Tất sat very still. He suddenly remembered himself two years earlier, at the second row of desks, quietly fixing what other people had broken, closing tickets, then going home. Back then, he too had thought the best people were the ones who didn’t need anyone.

On Wednesday evening, he messaged Trung and told him about the two requests. This time he didn’t whittle his words. He wrote straight out what he had been thinking for two days: *In a management book I read, they say you have to get the right people on the bus, and the wrong people off the bus. I think maybe Phong isn’t right for this team.*

Trung replied close to midnight:

*I’m tied up all this week, I can’t call in the evenings. I’ll just ask one question: have you asked Phong which seat on the bus he wants?*

> **Theory corner – First who, then what**
>
> In 2001, Jim Collins published *Good to Great*, the result of five years of research into companies that had made the leap from good to great and sustained it for at least fifteen years. One of his best-known findings is wrapped in the image of a bus: the leaders of those companies did not begin by deciding where to go. They began by getting the right people on the bus, the wrong people off the bus, and each person into the right seat. Only then did they work out the direction.
>
> People tend to remember the “off the bus” half and forget the “right seat” half. Collins stressed both. A talented person in the wrong seat can look exactly like someone who doesn’t fit. Telling those two cases apart is one of the hardest things a team leader has to do.

## A Week of Empty Desks

Before Thắng Tất had a chance to ask Phong anything, Phong was away.

It was the first time in three years Phong had taken leave: his younger sister was getting married, in a mountain district eight hours by bus from the city. He left on Wednesday evening, leaving exactly one line in the shared channel: *Off until Monday. System’s fine.*

That same week, Trung was away. The entire twelfth floor was closed for three days for the quarterly board meeting. Thắng Tất only knew this from a small notice taped up in the elevator, and because the café on the ground floor ran out of pastries earlier than usual. He thought to himself: why was everyone away this week?

At one forty on Friday morning, Thắng Tất’s phone buzzed.

It was Quân, who was in charge of the monitoring board, sending three messages in a row. For the past two days, nineteen clinics newly signed up for the Sound Sleep package had stopped receiving the half-past-eleven test call. And if the test call wasn’t running, Quân wrote, then quite possibly the call to the second contact wasn’t running either.

That was exactly the part Thư had proposed, after the six calls that rang in silence. The part Phong had rewritten, alone, on the night before he left.

Thắng Tất called Phong. The number could not be reached. He sent a message. It sat there with a single grey tick.

By half past two, five people were at the office, in a dark building with only one row of lights still on. They opened the part Phong had written. More than three thousand lines, running very fast, very clean, and not a single line explaining why it had been written that way. Reading it was like walking into a beautiful house that had no light switches.

![Five people in the dark building](Minh_hoa_Ch7_Nam_nguoi_trong_toa_nha_toi.jpg)

Ngọc Mai came last, carrying a bag of bánh mì from the bakery at the head of the lane, the only place with its lights still on. She couldn’t read code. She sat down next to Thắng Tất and, every half hour or so, topped up his cup without asking anything. Only toward dawn did he notice that his cup had never once run dry.

The one who found the bug, at nearly five in the morning, was Khoa.

He wasn’t better than anyone else. He was simply the only person in the room who had already read that code before, over and over, very slowly, because nobody would explain it to him and he didn’t want to ask again. A variable had been renamed in the last rewrite, and nineteen new clinics had fallen into a branch that nothing called anymore.

At seven in the morning, nineteen doctors received a late test call along with a message of apology. No patient was affected. Luckily, there had been no case that night that needed an urgent call.

Thư was the one who wrote the incident report. In the “what-if-it-fails” column, she filled in a line nobody in the room wanted to read aloud: *First to find out: luck.*

> **Theory corner – The intelligence of a group**
>
> In 2010, Anita Woolley, Christopher Chabris, Alex Pentland, Nada Hashmi and Thomas Malone published a study in the journal *Science* involving nearly seven hundred people, divided into small groups that worked together on many different kinds of problems. They found that each group had something that could be called “collective intelligence”, and that it did not depend much on the intelligence of the group’s smartest member, nor on the members’ average.
>
> What mattered more was how the group worked together: how sensitive the members were to the people beside them, and whether everyone got a turn to speak, or just a few people drowned out the rest. One very talented person doing all the work can make a group move faster for a while. But it can also make the whole group, measured by that very yardstick, less intelligent.

## Which Seat on the Bus

Phong got back to the office on Monday morning, his eyes still red from the overnight bus. He read the incident report from start to finish, standing, not sitting. When he reached Thư’s line, he stood still for a while.

Thắng Tất invited him down to the café on the ground floor. He didn’t bring the two requests, and he didn’t bring a solution either.

“I’m not planning to talk about last week,” Thắng Tất began. “Everyone makes mistakes. I want to ask you something else: if you could choose, what would you want to do on this team?”

Phong looked at his cup of coffee, then out through the glass door. He was silent for so long that Thắng Tất thought he wasn’t going to answer.

“I want to do the hardest parts,” Phong said. “And I want not to have to explain. Explaining takes three times as long as doing.”

“Last week, five people spent nearly three hours understanding a piece you did in one night.” Thắng Tất spoke slowly, without blame, just setting the number on the table the way Trung always did. “If it weren’t for Khoa, maybe nobody would understand it even now.”

Phong said nothing.

“In the book Trung always carries around, there’s a passage about choosing people,” Thắng Tất went on. “The writer puts talent third. Before talent come heart and mind. Under heart there are a few very simple questions: are they honest, do they give their all, do they shy away from hard work, and do they think of themselves or the group first.” He paused for a moment. “You can answer all of them, except the last. And I don’t think you’re selfish. I think you’ve never had to think about that question, because in three years on the night shift you had no group to think about.”

Phong looked up. For the first time, Thắng Tất saw something in his eyes that looked like surprise.

The night before, Thắng Tất had read another passage in the book, one he had to read three times before it stopped bothering him. The writer said that talented people, in the course of showing their talent, reveal a great many weaknesses, many Achilles’ heels, and a single small arrow is enough to kill them. Who will protect the talented? The organization, the leader.

He had meant to put Phong off the bus. He had very nearly done it. And he understood that if he had, he wouldn’t have been protecting the team. He would have been the one firing that small arrow.

On the very next page was a passage about rotating people that he had skimmed past before. The writer said that to know whether someone fits, you have to put them to real work. Anyone who doesn’t fit in one place should be moved to where they can do their best, sideways or up or down, rather than cast aside. Stay too long in one place and people wear down, and changing seats is itself a way of learning. But the move has to come from the person being moved. Reading that, he thought at once of Phong’s three years on the night shift.

“Here’s what I propose,” Thắng Tất said. “You keep doing the hardest parts. Nobody touches your work unless you want them to. But a piece of work only counts as done when someone else on the team can fix it at three in the morning without having to call you. The first person to learn with you is Khoa.”

He paused for a moment, then added: “There’s one more thing, if you agree. For the next three months, you try the day shift. Lực Sỹ has offered to take the night shift in your place. He says three years in one seat is too long for anyone, even the best. You can say no.”

Phong was silent for a long while.

“The day shift is fine,” he said at last. “But the kid already asked to transfer.”

“Not yet,” Thắng Tất said. “I haven’t signed it.”

## Five Fingers

Khoa stayed. Tùng didn’t.

Thắng Tất sat with Tùng for nearly an hour, told him about his proposal, asked whether he would like to try one more month. Tùng heard him out, thanked him, and still asked to leave. He said one thing that Thắng Tất would never forget: “I believe you’ll fix it. But I was worn out before you started fixing it.”

Thắng Tất signed the request. He didn’t try to hold him any longer. He understood that when a team leader is three months late, someone pays for those three months, and this time the one who paid was Tùng.

That weekend, Phong posted a four-page document in the shared channel, explaining the code that five people had spent nearly three hours reading. At the bottom was a small note: *Written with Khoa. The parts that make sense are Khoa’s.* Nobody commented. But by evening, everyone in the company had opened it, and some had read it three times.

On Friday afternoon, Trung came back to the office after more than a week away. Thắng Tất told him everything, including the part he had nearly gotten wrong.

Trung heard it all, then turned the old book to a page Thắng Tất had never read and swivelled it toward him:

*Of the five fingers, some are short and some are long, but each has its own function, and that is why, when they close together, they make a fist.*

“People tend to read this as being about unity,” Trung said. “To me it’s about not making the thumb be as long as the middle finger. And not cutting off the thumb just because it isn’t like the others.”

“But I still lost Tùng.”

“Yes.” Trung nodded. “Every team leader has a name like that. Don’t forget it. It will make you faster next time.”

Before leaving, Thắng Tất told him one more thing: he wanted to bring back the seat-changing custom Lực Sỹ had mentioned. Each quarter, one person on the team would change places, sideways, up or down, as long as that person wanted to.

Trung listened, then nodded. “There’s a passage in that book I like,” he said. “Seen from the outside, some people move up, some move down, some move sideways. But wherever they land, they have to prove themselves all over again. If they can, then even from the lowest place there is still a way up.” He took a sip of his coffee. “People tend to read that as a consolation for those who get moved down. I read it as advice for someone who chooses the harder seat, too.”

Thắng Tất guessed he was talking about Lực Sỹ, the longest-serving member of the team, who had just volunteered to take the night shift alone.

> **Theory corner – One bad apple**
>
> In 2006, Will Felps, Terence Mitchell and Eliza Byington published a review in *Research in Organizational Behavior* on how one negative member can weaken an entire group. They described three types: the person who doesn’t pull their weight, the person who is constantly in a negative mood, and the person who treats colleagues with disrespect. The impact of one such person is often much greater than the positive impact of one good member, because the others become defensive, withdraw, or start to imitate them.
>
> But the authors also pointed out that behavior often comes from context more than from character. Phong didn’t lack effort, nor did he lack respect. He had simply carried the habits of the night shift into a daytime room. The question for a team leader is not only whether this person fits, but where you have put them, and whether you have told them the rules of the game there.

## Thắng Tất’s Notebook

He wrote this page on the night he signed Tùng’s request:

1. **A talented person in the wrong seat looks a lot like someone who doesn’t fit.** Before you ask anyone off the bus, ask them which seat they want, and whether anyone has told them the rules of this bus. And don’t let anyone sit in one seat too long, not even the best.
2. **Choosing people: heart first, mind second, talent third.** The question under heart that is easiest to skip is this: do they think of themselves or the group first. Some people can’t answer it because they have never had a group to think about.
3. **Protecting talented people is the team leader’s job. So is protecting the whole team.** Be slow at either one, and someone will pay. Remember their name.

In the margin, he wrote small: *A piece of work is only done when someone else can fix it at three in the morning.*
